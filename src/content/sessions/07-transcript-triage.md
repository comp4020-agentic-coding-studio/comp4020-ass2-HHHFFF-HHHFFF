Practice run for the assessment, on a transcript that is deliberately not
yours. Your own is due Friday.

## Before you arrive

Have your allocated transcript from week 6 open, but do not bring your
analysis of it. Today is somebody else's exchange.

## In the session

**Solo markup, twenty-five minutes.** A shared transcript, marked up
individually and silently. Every deviation you can find, with the standard
form written next to it.

**Pairs, twenty minutes.** Swap. The task is not to agree — it is to find
where you disagree and say why. Two common patterns show up:

- One of you marked something the other did not. Usually a real deviation,
  spotted by whoever knows that phrase better.
- One of you marked something that is not a deviation at all. Almost always an
  accent, a regional form, or a controller clipping words under load.

**The triage table, thirty minutes.** As a room, every marked item goes into
one of three columns:

1. **Deviation from the standard** — name the phrase it should have been.
2. **Delivery** — accent, speed, clipping, hesitation. Not a deviation. Not a
   defect.
3. **Arguable** — and it stays there.

Column 2 is the one that teaches. Most first attempts at this assessment lose
marks in column 2: the most *unusual-sounding* transmission gets diagnosed,
and it turns out to be a non-native speaker using entirely correct
phraseology, while the actual deviation was fluent, friendly and completely
standard-sounding.

Column 3 is not a failure either. "Arguable" with a reason is a better answer
than a confident miscategorisation, and the assessment marks it that way.

**Harm, last fifteen minutes.** For three items from column 1: what did this
risk *in this exchange*? Not in general. The assessment asks for the specific
harm and this is where people discover that some real deviations risked
nothing much on the day, which is an honest finding and worth writing.

## A note on column 2

We are strict about this for a reason that is week 8's subject. Marking a
foreign accent as a defect is both wrong on the facts and a thing that happens
to real pilots with real consequences. If your analysis diagnoses a speaker
rather than a form, it will be marked down, and Priya will say so directly
because she is the person who assesses this professionally.

## Afterwards

The transcript analysis is due Friday noon. Bring questions to the frequency
or email — but note the marking criteria reward a checkable finding over a
clever one, so if you are choosing between the two, choose checkable.
