Current first officer, and a rated ICAO language-proficiency examiner: one of
the people who sits across a desk and decides whether a pilot's English is
good enough to be allowed on a frequency.

Runs the sim sessions and writes the drills. The drills are built from real
transmissions rather than invented ones, which is why they contain the
hesitations, the stepped-on calls and the mid-phrase corrections that a
textbook example never has.

## What to ask about

The examiner's side of week 8: what Level 4 actually requires, what it feels
like to assess it, and the uncomfortable fact that a rating scale for human
language has consequences for people's careers.

Also the thing students ask most: how much of this matters once you are
actually flying. The honest answer is that most of it is invisible until the
day it isn't, and that the drills are for that day.

## On being assessed by a practitioner

Marking your drills is not the same as flying with you, and it would be easy
to blur those. The marking criteria on each assessment page are the ones that
apply — not "would I want this person on my frequency", which is a judgement
this course has no business making about a first-year student.
