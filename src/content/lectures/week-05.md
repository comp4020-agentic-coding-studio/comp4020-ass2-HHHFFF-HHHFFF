On 27 March 1977, at Los Rodeos on Tenerife, a KLM 747 began its take-off roll
in fog without a take-off clearance. A Pan Am 747 was backtracking the same
runway. Both aircraft were destroyed. 583 people died. 61 survived, all from
the Pan Am aircraft.

You already hold the rule from week 4. Today the accident is evidence.

## Why both aircraft were there at all

Neither was scheduled into Tenerife. Both were bound for Las Palmas on Gran
Canaria, which closed after a bomb detonated in the terminal. Los Rodeos took
the diversions, and a small airport filled with widebodies parked on its
taxiway.

Which is why the Pan Am aircraft was on the runway: with the taxiway
obstructed, departing aircraft had to backtrack the runway and turn off part
way down. And Los Rodeos sits at about 2,000 ft, where hill fog arrives
quickly. Visibility was falling through the sequence.

This matters for the course's argument. **The phraseology was operating
outside the conditions it was designed for** — non-routine ground movement, no
ground radar, aircraft that could not see each other, and a crew under
commercial pressure over duty hours. A designed language has an envelope, and
this was outside it.

## The four points where the language failed

**One. A non-standard progress report.** The Pan Am crew were asked to report
when clear of the runway. The KLM crew, holding at the end, transmitted a
report of their own status using the construction week 4 dissected:

```atc-nonstandard
KLM      We are now at takeoff.
```

Ambiguous between *positioned at the takeoff point* and *commencing takeoff*.
The controller, expecting the first, replied with a phrase that in the
phraseology of the day was not a clearance and not a prohibition:

```atc-nonstandard
TWR      OK. Stand by for takeoff, I will call you.
```

`OK` has no defined meaning. It arrived first, and it arrived as
acknowledgement of whatever the KLM crew believed they had said.

**Two. A simultaneous transmission.** As the tower transmitted "stand by for
takeoff, I will call you", the Pan Am crew transmitted that they were still
taxiing on the runway. The two overlapped. In the KLM cockpit the critical
portion was obscured — a heterodyne where the prohibition should have been.

This is week 1's simplex channel, doing exactly what week 1 said it does. No
transmitting station could know it had happened.

**Three. The reserved word, used outside its clearance, by ATC.** "Stand by
for takeoff" contains the word. In fog, partly stepped on, a transmission
containing `TAKEOFF` reached a crew already committed to going.

**Four. A challenge that was not pressed.** The KLM flight engineer asked
whether the Pan Am aircraft was clear. He was answered in the affirmative by
both pilots and did not ask again. That is a crew-resource-management finding
rather than a phraseology one, and this course is careful about the boundary:
there was no phrase available to him for *I believe the clearance is invalid*.
Week 12 comes back to whether there should be.

## What changed afterwards

The recommendations emphasised standard phraseology in all normal
communications, and specifically the avoidance of "take-off" in departure
clearances. That is the direct ancestor of the rule you learned last week.

## What to be careful about

Tenerife is the most-told accident in aviation, and the telling has smoothed
it. Two cautions:

- **It is one accident.** The rule it produced is broader than one accident
  can justify. That does not make the rule wrong — it makes the justification
  partly something other than this accident, and you should be able to say
  what.
- **The KLM captain has become a character in a story.** The investigation did
  attribute the accident primarily to his actions. A course about language
  design has nothing useful to say about his personality, and quite a lot to
  say about a system in which the only available word for *stop* was one that
  contained the word *takeoff*.

Read the ALPA human factors report alongside the findings. It disagrees with
the Spanish report in places, and the disagreement is instructive.
