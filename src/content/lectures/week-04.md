In this language, one word is reserved. `TAKE-OFF` may be used in exactly two
places: the clearance that grants it, and the cancellation that withdraws it.

Everywhere else — the clearance to the departure end, the request, the
briefing, the readback of a route, the phrase for the phase of flight — the
word is **`DEPARTURE`**.

```atc
TWR      Speedbird one two three, runway two seven left, cleared for take-off.
BAW123   Cleared for take-off, runway two seven left, Speedbird one two three.
```

That is the only shape it appears in. And:

```atc
TWR      Speedbird one two three, hold position, cancel take-off, I say again,
         cancel take-off. Acknowledge.
BAW123   Holding position, Speedbird one two three.
```

## Why reserve a word at all

Because the channel loses words, and a lost word changes a sentence's meaning
without changing its plausibility.

Consider a station that says it is *at takeoff*. In ordinary English that is
ambiguous between "positioned at the takeoff point, waiting" and "in the act
of taking off". Both readings are grammatical. Both are things an aircraft
could truthfully be. And a controller expecting the first, hearing a partly
degraded transmission, has no way to tell.

The fix is not to clarify the phrase. It is to make the word unavailable, so
that a transmission containing it can only be one of two things. Any other
appearance is immediately anomalous — and *anomalous* is a state a busy human
can detect, where *ambiguous* is not.

This is a general move, and worth naming: **the design does not try to make
the ambiguous message clearer. It removes the ambiguous message from the
language.**

## The forms that are wrong

```atc-nonstandard
TWR      Speedbird one two three, taxi into position and hold.
BAW123   Position and hold, Speedbird one two three.
```

`POSITION AND HOLD` is a legacy North American form. ICAO is `LINE UP AND
WAIT`, and the reason for the change is exactly this week's argument: *hold*
is doing too many jobs, and a clearance to enter a runway should not share
vocabulary with an instruction to stop.

```atc-nonstandard
BAW123   Tower, Speedbird one two three, we are now at takeoff.
```

This is the one that matters. It uses the reserved word outside a clearance,
in the ambiguous construction, as a progress report — a category of message
that has no defined form at all.

## Why the rule comes before the accident

Next week is Tenerife, and that transmission is from it.

We deliberately do the doctrine first. If you meet the accident first, the
rule arrives as its moral, and a moral is easy to accept and hard to argue
with. Doing it in this order means that next week you already hold the rule,
and the accident functions as **evidence** — including evidence about which
parts of the rule the accident actually supports and which parts got attached
to it afterwards.

That distinction is real and you will be asked to make it. The Tenerife
investigation's recommendations did emphasise standard phraseology and did
call for avoiding the phrase "take-off" in departure clearances. They are also
one accident, and the rule as it now stands is broader than one accident can
justify. Holding both of those at once is the skill this course is for.
