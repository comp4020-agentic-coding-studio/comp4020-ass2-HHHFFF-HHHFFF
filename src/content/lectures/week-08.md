Everything so far has been about the language. This week is about the people
required to speak it, and the machinery that decides whether they may.

## Why a scale exists at all

Phraseology covers the routine. The moment a situation is not routine, the
phrase book runs out and the speakers fall back on **plain language** — and
plain language is where proficiency stops being a formality.

This is the argument that produced regulation. Charkhi Dadri, from week 6, is
one of its sources: fifteen recommendations, several of them specifically on
English language proficiency. Avianca 052, from week 7, is another — a crew
who said true things in a form that did not carry their urgency.

So ICAO built a rating scale, six levels across six criteria: pronunciation,
structure, vocabulary, fluency, comprehension, and interactions. **Level 4 is
operational**, the minimum to work internationally. Below it you do not fly
international. Level 4 requires periodic re-testing; Level 6 does not.

Note the shape of the thing. It is not a test of English. It is a test of
whether you can handle the *unexpected* in English, which is a much stranger
thing to measure.

## What Level 4 actually says

Read the descriptors rather than the number. Level 4 does not require an
accent-free speaker, or a fluent one, or one who never makes an error. It
requires, roughly, that pronunciation is intelligible to the aeronautical
community, that errors do not interfere with meaning, that the speaker can
paraphrase when a word is missing, and that comprehension holds up when the
exchange takes an unexpected turn.

That last criterion is the one that does the work, and the one hardest to
assess in a booked twenty-minute interview.

## The uncomfortable part

Priya assesses this professionally, and this lecture is co-taught so that the
uncomfortable part gets said by the person who does it rather than about them.

A rating scale for human language has consequences for people's careers, and
those consequences do not fall evenly:

- **The scale is applied to non-native speakers in practice.** A native
  speaker with genuinely poor radio discipline is rarely the one being
  re-tested.
- **Native speakers are a hazard the scale does not measure.** Idiom, speed
  and local shorthand from a native speaker are a well-documented
  comprehension problem for everybody else on frequency — and there is no
  rating that catches it. Week 7's catalogue was mostly native-speaker
  behaviour.
- **Testing is uneven** across states and providers, and the incentive to
  award Level 4 sits with people who are not neutral.
- **Accent is not the criterion, and gets marked anyway.** Which is why this
  course refuses to mark it, and says so on every assessment page.

## Plain language, and doing it well

The practical skill: when the phrase book runs out, say the thing plainly and
slowly, and structure it. `MAYDAY` first if it is one. Nature of the problem,
intentions, assistance required. Short sentences. One idea each.

```atc
BAW123   Mayday, mayday, mayday, Speedbird one two three, engine failure,
         one two thousand feet, turning left towards the coast,
         request immediate descent and vectors to nearest suitable.
```

Standard where the standard exists, plain where it does not, and no attempt to
sound calm at the cost of being clear. Sounding calm is not a criterion
either.

## The thing to take away

The scale is a real answer to a real problem, and it is also a blunt
instrument pointed mostly at one group of people. Both. If your instinct is
that it should be abolished, say what replaces it for the crew who cannot
handle a non-routine exchange. If your instinct is that it should be
stricter, say what catches the native speaker.
