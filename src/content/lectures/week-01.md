Ordinary English is extraordinarily good at its job. It is redundant, so you
can lose half of it and still recover the meaning. It repairs itself: if a
listener looks puzzled you say it differently, and you know within a syllable
whether you were understood. It is expressive enough that two people can
negotiate meaning they did not have words for when they started.

Aviation radio strips almost all of that away.

## What the channel takes from you

The frequency is **simplex**: one carrier, one speaker. Press to transmit and
you cannot hear, so you cannot know you have been stepped on. Two stations
transmitting together produce a heterodyne whine, or — worse — one clean
transmission and one that was never heard by anybody, including the person who
sent it.

There is no face. No puzzled expression, no raised eyebrow, none of the
back-channel that ordinary conversation runs on. You get one modality, and it
is noisy: VHF over a jet cabin, through a headset, at altitude.

The listener is frequently not a native speaker of your language, is
frequently tired, and is frequently doing three other things. So are you.

And the exchange is often one-shot. A clearance acted on wrongly is not a
misunderstanding to be sorted out later; it is an aircraft somewhere it should
not be, now.

## What the design does about it

The response, worked out across the second half of the twentieth century, was
not to teach everybody better English. It was to build a much smaller language
and make it mandatory.

- **A closed vocabulary.** A few hundred words and phrases with single defined
  meanings. `SAY AGAIN` means one thing. It does not also mean *I disagree*,
  the way "sorry?" does in English.
- **Fixed word order.** Callsign, then instruction, then constraint. You know
  what slot the next word is in before you hear it, which is what lets you
  parse a transmission you only half-received.
- **Mandatory readback.** The listener says the instruction back. The channel
  has no acknowledgement, so one is built out of the message itself.
- **Reserved words.** A handful of words are forbidden except in one exact
  context. Week 4 is about a single one of them.

Note what all four have in common: each one gives up expressiveness on
purpose. That is the trade. This language is worse than English at almost
everything, in exchange for being harder to get wrong at one thing.

## The claim this course makes

Phraseology is usually taught as a list to memorise, as though it had always
been there. It hasn't. Every phrase in it was **chosen**, by identifiable
people, out of alternatives, on evidence that was sometimes good and sometimes
a single accident. Somebody argued for `AFFIRM` over `AFFIRMATIVE` and won.

So this course reads phraseology as a designed artefact. For each rule: what
problem was it for, what was the alternative, what evidence decided it, and
does the evidence still hold. That last question is live — week 10 is about a
technology that removes the mishearing problem entirely, and takes something
else away with it.

The accident record is the other half. It is not here for drama. It is the
only evidence available about where a language engineered against
misunderstanding still lets misunderstanding through.

## What a transmission looks like

Written out, with the station on the left. This is the notation the whole site
uses:

```atc
TWR      Speedbird one two three, wind two four zero degrees one two knots,
         runway two seven left, cleared to land.
BAW123   Cleared to land, runway two seven left, Speedbird one two three.
```

Two lines. Every element in the readback appears in the clearance, and nothing
appears in the readback that was not in the clearance. Get used to reading
these that way — as a pair to be checked against each other, not as two people
talking.
