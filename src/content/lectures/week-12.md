## What the twelve weeks claimed

That ICAO phraseology is a **designed artefact**: authored, argued over,
revised, and answerable to evidence. Not a tradition, not a list, not
common sense written down.

And that it works. The reserved word, the closed vocabulary, the mandatory
readback, the respelled digits — these are real engineering against a real
channel, and the accident rate over the period they were introduced is not a
coincidence.

## What it cannot do

**It cannot make the sentence type survive.** Charkhi Dadri. The digits
arrived; the difference between *information* and *instruction* did not.
Readback tests the content and nothing tests the type.

**It cannot supply urgency the speaker withholds.** Avianca 052. The phrase
existed. Having a word for the emergency does not make somebody say it.

**It cannot close a loop that both parties close correctly on false
premises.** Air Canada 759. Question answered accurately, about the wrong
runway, by a controller with no way to know.

**It cannot cover the non-routine.** Which is why plain language exists, and
why week 8's rating scale exists, and why the hardest part of speaking on a
frequency is the part with no phrase for it.

**And it has no phrase for doubt.**

## The empty row

Week 5, the mapping table, the row that never fills: the KLM flight engineer
asked whether the other aircraft was clear, was answered, and did not ask
again.

Fifty years on, there is still no standard phrase for *I believe the clearance
we are acting on is not valid.*

Consider what such a phrase would need to do. It has to be sayable by a junior
crew member to a senior one, on an open frequency, in front of everybody,
without being an accusation. It has to be short. It has to be
unmisinterpretable. And it has to compel a response rather than invite one.

Aviation has attacked this problem hard, and almost entirely **outside**
phraseology: crew resource management, the two-challenge rule, explicit
authority-gradient training. The industry decided this was a culture problem
rather than a vocabulary problem.

Is that right? Three positions, and you have heard all three this semester:

- **Halvard:** a phrase would be ignored under the same authority gradient
  that silenced the challenge. You cannot vocabulary your way out of a
  hierarchy.
- **Priya:** a phrase gives a junior speaker cover. Saying a designated form
  is much easier than composing an objection, because the form carries the
  authority instead of the speaker.
- **Noor:** the fact that fifty years of design attention has not produced one
  is itself the evidence — and we should be suspicious of a gap that everyone
  agrees is a gap and nobody fills.

Pick one on Wednesday. You will be asked.

## Why the audit went outside aviation

Because the argument either transfers or it was never an argument.

The reasoning you have — find the channel, find what it loses, find where the
language is ambiguous, remove the ambiguous form rather than clarifying it,
and ask what the messy version was doing for free — is not about aeroplanes.
It is about any group of people speaking under pressure on a channel that
loses things, which is most of the important talking anybody does.

Your audits are the evidence about whether you have it. They are also, most
years, better than the aviation examples at showing where the reasoning breaks
down, because nobody has smoothed those domains into a story yet.

## One last thing

The most useful two words in this language are `SAY AGAIN`, and the reason is
not that they are clear. It is that they are **cheap**. A system where
admitting you did not understand costs you nothing is a system that finds out
about misunderstandings while they are still cheap.

That is the design principle worth taking out of here, and it has almost
nothing to do with aviation.
