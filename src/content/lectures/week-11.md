A methods lecture. Less argument, more craft, because the audit is due this
week and the difference between a good one and a bad one is almost entirely
method.

## Transcribing honestly

**Mark what you cannot hear.** `[unintelligible]`, with a duration. This is
the single most important habit and the one students resist, because a
transcript full of gaps feels like a failure. It is the opposite: a gap is
data, and a confident guess is contamination that the next reader cannot
detect.

**Transcribe what was said, not what was meant.** If a speaker said *thirteen
thousand*, write `thirteen thousand`. Normalising it to `ONE THREE THOUSAND`
destroys the finding.

**Separate the transcript from the analysis, physically.** Two documents. Once
you are annotating as you transcribe, you start hearing the annotation.

**Say how many passes you made,** and at what speed. A transcript is a
measurement and measurements come with methods.

**Timestamp everything.** Overlaps are only visible with timings, and overlaps
are this course's subject.

## Diagnosing against a standard

The failure mode is diagnosing *vibes*. The discipline is three steps, in
order, every time:

1. **Quote the transmission.** Exactly.
2. **Name the standard form** it departs from, and where that standard lives —
   the glossary, ICAO phraseology, a local supplement.
3. **State the risk in this exchange.** Not the general risk of the deviation.
   What could have gone wrong here, given who was on frequency and what they
   were doing.

If step 2 comes up empty, you do not have a deviation. You may have something
real — a delivery problem, an equipment problem, a workload problem — and you
should say which, but do not call it a phraseology finding.

Step 3 is where honest analyses admit that a genuine deviation risked very
little on the day. Write that. It is a stronger finding than an inflated one,
and it is marked as such.

## Writing a checkable finding

A finding is checkable when a reader who thinks you are wrong can locate
everything you relied on and evaluate it without asking you.

That means:

- The quote is there, not summarised.
- The standard is cited, not gestured at.
- The inferential step — from *what was said* to *what it risked* — is
  visible as a separate claim, so it can be attacked separately.
- Your uncertainty is stated where you have it.

The last one is the mark of the thing. "I could not make out the level in the
readback, so I cannot say whether it matched" is a better sentence than any
confident alternative, and every accident report worth reading is full of
sentences like it.

## Findings versus blame

Annex 13 separates investigation from liability: the purpose is prevention,
not attribution. This course borrows the posture wholesale.

Write about the transmission and the system that produced it. Do not write
about the character of the person transmitting. This is not squeamishness —
it is that "the captain was arrogant" has no design consequence, while "the
only available word for stop contained the word takeoff" has one, and the
second is what a report is for.

## Applying it to your own domain

The audit asks you to do all of this somewhere nobody has done it for you.
There is no glossary for clinical handover, which means step 2 has no standard
to name — so you must first establish what the current protocol *is*, from
observation, before you can call anything a departure from it.

That is the hard part of the audit, and it is where the 30% for evidence sits.
Do not skip to proposing phrases. A proposal built on what you imagine people
say is worth nothing, and it reads exactly like what it is.
