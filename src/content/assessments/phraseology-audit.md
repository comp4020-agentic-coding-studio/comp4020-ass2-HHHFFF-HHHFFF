> Find a place outside aviation where people speak to each other under
> pressure on a channel that loses things. Audit it. Propose a phraseology.

This is the largest piece of work in the course and the only one that leaves
aviation. That is deliberate: a course about one designed language is worth
very little if the reasoning does not transfer, and the way to find out
whether you have the reasoning is to take it somewhere it has not been done
for you.

## Choosing a domain

It has to be a real protocol, with real speakers, that you can actually
observe or read transcripts of. Candidates that have worked:

- **Clinical handover** — nursing shift handover, ambulance-to-ED handoff,
  surgical count. Some of these already have designed protocols; auditing an
  existing one is entirely fair game and often stronger.
- **Rail** — signaller-to-driver exchanges, degraded-mode working.
- **Maritime** — bridge-to-bridge, pilotage, VTS.
- **Incident response** — the conference bridge on a major software outage,
  where the channel is voice, the participants are tired, and nobody can see
  each other.
- **Fireground and emergency dispatch.**

What does not work: a domain you cannot get evidence about, or a domain with
no pressure in it. A café order is a protocol but nothing is lost when it
fails.

Clear your choice by week 9. Two people may take the same domain; you will be
marked separately and you may not share evidence.

## What you submit

**An audit**, 1500–2000 words. What the protocol is, who speaks, what the
channel loses, where the current language is ambiguous, and what the
consequences of ambiguity are.

**A proposed phraseology.** A table of phrases with defined meanings. For each
one: the failure it is designed against. A phrase with no named failure is
decoration and will be marked as such.

**A limits section.** Explicitly: how could this proposal make things worse?

## How it is marked

**Fit of the chosen domain (15).** Is there real pressure, a real channel, and
real evidence available?

**Evidence gathered (30).** First-hand observation, published transcripts, or
documented incidents. Not what you imagine a nurse says. If you observed, say
when, where, how much, and what you could not see.

**The proposed phraseology (35).** Judged on whether each phrase is designed
against a failure you actually demonstrated, whether the set is small enough
to learn, and whether it removes ambiguity rather than relabelling it.

**Honesty about limits (20).** Twenty per cent, because it is the criterion
most likely to be skipped and the one that separates a design proposal from
advocacy. Every constraint this course admires costs something: expressiveness,
speed, the ability to say a thing the vocabulary does not cover. A proposal
that claims no cost has not been thought about.

The strongest submissions in this criterion have argued that their own
proposal should not be adopted.

## Ethics

If you observe people at work, you need their knowledge and consent, and you
must not record without it. Handover conversations contain patient
information; do not transcribe it. If your domain requires an ethics
application, it is the wrong domain for a twelve-week course — choose
published transcripts instead. Ask in week 9 if you are unsure.
